Every harbor town remembers its ships in a different way than the sea does. The sea keeps no ledger; it simply returns what it is given, sometimes gently and sometimes not at all. The town, by contrast, writes everything down, though never in one place. Part of the record lives in the tide charts nailed above the harbor master's desk, part in the names painted on the sterns of boats that no longer sail, and part in the memory of the old men who sit on the seawall each morning and argue about weather that has not yet arrived. A stranger walking the quay at dawn would see only rope and rust and the slow business of nets being mended. He would not see the second town underneath, the one made of routes and habits, the shortcut through the cannery yard that everyone uses and no map admits, the corner where the wind always turns cold an hour before the fog rolls in. To read that town you must stay through a season, or better through several, until the printed map and the walked map begin to overlap in your own legs. Then the harbor stops being a picture and becomes a sentence you can finish before it is spoken. The fishermen know this without saying it. They read the water the way a scholar reads a margin, attentive to what is written faintly and almost erased. When a young deckhand asks how they know a squall is coming, they rarely give a reason that would satisfy an instrument. They point instead at the color of the far swell, or the way the gulls have gone quiet, or a smell that has no name in any language the boy has been taught. These are not superstitions but a kind of compression, years of mornings folded into a single glance. The instruments on the newer boats are more precise and less wise; they measure the present exactly and forecast the future poorly, because they have no season stored in them. And so the town keeps both maps, the paper and the remembered, and trusts neither entirely. On calm days the paper wins, and the harbor runs on schedule and paperwork. On the days when the barometer falls and the light goes strange, the older map takes over, and men who cannot explain themselves are suddenly the only ones worth listening to. The harbor survives because it never fully commits to either kind of knowing, holding the measured and the felt in the same loose hand, the way a good sailor holds a line: firmly enough not to lose it, loosely enough to let it run.
